When buying a home, the furnace, roof, and kitchen get inspected three times over — but it's the invisible legacies that often decide five-figure follow-up costs. The good news: **pollutants follow the construction year.** If you know when a house was built and renovated, you know what to look for.

## The checklist by era

| Built / era | Typical pollutants |
| --- | --- |
| before 1978 | **Lead paint** — the seller must disclose it under federal law; deteriorating paint and window friction surfaces are the risk |
| before ~1986 | Lead pipes and lead solder in plumbing — see [Lead in drinking water](/en/articles/lead-in-drinking-water) |
| before ~1980 | **Asbestos** in ceilings, floors, insulation, adhesives — the top candidate, see [Asbestos at home](/en/articles/asbestos-at-home) |
| ~1965–1990 | Formaldehyde from particleboard and paneling; vermiculite attic insulation |
| any year | **Radon** depending on location and construction ([check your area](/en/articles/what-is-radon)), mold from moisture damage |

Important: an older home can be renovated and clean — and an '80s house can be full of surprises. The table tells you **where to look harder**, not what you'll definitely find.

## How to proceed

1. **Request the paperwork:** year built, renovation history, prior inspection reports, the federally required lead-paint disclosure (pre-1978 homes). Serious sellers have this ready; evasion is a signal.
2. **Walk the property with pollutant glasses on:** popcorn ceilings, 9×9 vinyl tiles, old pipe wrap, musty basement smell, water stains — note and photograph everything.
3. **On suspicion: test before you sign.** A radon test is standard practice in U.S. real estate transactions; asbestos and lead samples cost $30–75 each at accredited labs; a specialized environmental inspection runs a few hundred to a couple thousand dollars — a small insurance premium on the biggest purchase of your life.
4. **Negotiate findings into the price:** documented remediation needs are your best argument for a credit — or for walking away before it gets expensive.
5. **Plan the radon follow-up:** the short-term test from the transaction is a snapshot; put a [long-term test](/en/articles/testing-for-radon) on the list as your first act after moving in.

## The most expensive mistake

It isn't the overlooked pollutant — it's **renovating without checking first**. Whoever "just rips the walls out" of an older house spreads asbestos fibers and lead dust everywhere and turns a manageable professional job into a total loss. Test first, then build.
