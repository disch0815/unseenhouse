When buying a home, the furnace, roof, and kitchen get inspected three times over — but it's the invisible legacies that often decide five-figure follow-up costs. Undetected asbestos can add tens of thousands to a planned remodel; lead service lines can mean replacing the home's entire water supply. The good news: **pollutants follow the construction year.** Materials were used in specific eras and banned at specific dates — if you know when a house was built and renovated, you know what to look for.

## The checklist by era

| Built / era | Typical pollutants |
| --- | --- |
| before 1978 | **Lead paint** — the seller must disclose it under federal law; deteriorating paint and window friction surfaces are the risk |
| before ~1986 | Lead pipes and lead solder in plumbing — see [Lead in drinking water](/en/articles/lead-in-drinking-water) |
| before ~1980 | **Asbestos** in ceilings, floors, insulation, adhesives — the top candidate, see [Asbestos at home](/en/articles/asbestos-at-home) |
| ~1965–1990 | Formaldehyde from particleboard and paneling; vermiculite attic insulation |
| any year | **Radon** depending on location and construction ([check your area](/en/articles/what-is-radon)), mold from moisture damage |

Important: an older home can be renovated and clean — and an '80s house can be full of surprises. The table tells you **where to look harder**, not what you'll definitely find.

## The candidates in detail

**Lead paint (banned for residential use in 1978).** The headline risk in pre-1978 homes, especially for families with small children: deteriorating paint sheds lead dust, and friction surfaces — window sashes, door jambs, painted stairs — grind it into the household continuously. Federal law requires sellers of pre-1978 homes to disclose known lead paint and hand over the EPA pamphlet; buyers get a **10-day window for a lead inspection**, which is worth using. Intact paint under newer layers is a manageable, maintain-it problem; flaking paint plus toddlers is not.

**Asbestos (phased out through the 1980s).** The most expensive line item because it can hide in dozens of materials: popcorn ceilings, 9×9-inch vinyl floor tiles and their black mastic, pipe and duct insulation, cement siding and roofing, joint compounds. Intact, undisturbed asbestos poses little day-to-day risk — every drilling, sanding, and demolition job is where it gets critical. That makes asbestos primarily a **renovation-cost issue**: licensed abatement with containment and disposal costs a multiple of ordinary demolition. A special case is **vermiculite attic insulation** (brand name Zonolite): most of it is asbestos-contaminated, and the EPA's advice is simple — don't disturb it, price its removal in.

**Lead in water (pipes and solder to ~1986).** Lead service lines and lead solder were legal until the 1986 amendment of the Safe Drinking Water Act. Check the line where it enters the house: dull gray metal you can scratch shiny with a screwdriver means lead. Many utilities now publish **service line inventories** — mandated by the EPA's Lead and Copper Rule Improvements — so a lookup by address is often free. Details and testing: [Lead in drinking water](/en/articles/lead-in-drinking-water).

**Formaldehyde and old insulation (~1965–1990).** Particleboard, paneling, and cabinetry from this era used urea-formaldehyde glues that off-gas for years — a pungent smell in closed rooms is a hint. Modern boards (CARB Phase 2 / TSCA Title VI compliant) emit far less.

**Radon and mold — any year.** Radon depends on geology and the condition of the slab, not on age ([why, explained here](/en/articles/what-is-radon)). Mold follows moisture — water stains, musty basement smell, and freshly repainted basement corners tell you more during a walkthrough than any seller statement.

## How to proceed

1. **Request the paperwork:** year built, renovation history, prior inspection reports, the federally required lead-paint disclosure for pre-1978 homes. Serious sellers have this ready; evasion is a signal. Renovation years matter most: a 1930 house "modernized" in 1975 can contain asbestos materials the original build never had.
2. **Walk the property with pollutant glasses on:** popcorn ceilings, 9×9 vinyl tiles, old pipe wrap, vermiculite in the attic, the water line entering the basement, musty smells, water stains — note and photograph everything. Don't drill or break anything; sampling is the professional's job.
3. **On suspicion: test before you sign.** A **radon test is standard practice** in U.S. transactions — make it a contingency. Asbestos and lead samples cost **$30–75 each** at accredited labs; a specialized environmental inspection runs **a few hundred to about $2,000** depending on scope — a small insurance premium on the biggest purchase of your life. For lead, use EPA-certified risk assessors; for asbestos, state-licensed inspectors.
4. **Negotiate findings into the price:** a documented remediation need with a cost estimate is your best argument for a credit or price reduction — or for walking away before it gets expensive. Without documentation you're negotiating on gut feeling against a seller who knows their house.
5. **Plan the radon follow-up:** the short-term test from the transaction is a snapshot; put a [long-term test](/en/articles/testing-for-radon) on the list as your first act after moving in. If the house sits in an EPA Zone 1 county, budget a reserve for a mitigation system ($800–2,500).

## The most expensive mistake

It isn't the overlooked pollutant — it's **renovating without checking first**. Whoever "just rips the walls out" of an older house spreads asbestos fibers and lead dust through every room and turns a manageable professional job into a total loss with decontamination. Test first, then build. That includes seemingly harmless work: pulling up old flooring, scraping a popcorn ceiling, sanding painted windows — all classic release paths in pre-1980 homes. For any paid work on pre-1978 homes, contractors must be **EPA RRP-certified** (Renovation, Repair and Painting rule) — asking for the certificate is a two-minute quality filter.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does the seller have to disclose pollutants?

Lead paint in pre-1978 homes: yes, by federal law. Beyond that, disclosure duties vary by state — most require disclosing known material defects, but proving the seller knew is hard. Practical consequence: rely on your own inspection contingency, not on disclosure law.

### Is an environmental inspection worth it for a condo?

Yes, with a shifted focus: inside the unit it's about floors, ceilings, paint, and the bathroom; siding, roof, risers, and attic insulation belong to the association — so read the HOA minutes: has an asbestos survey ever been done? Are assessments looming for work with no reserves behind it?

### Is a house with asbestos unsellable or unlivable?

No. Intact, undisturbed asbestos (say, sound cement siding or tiles under carpet) can stay in place and poses little everyday risk — federal rules mostly regulate disturbance, not presence. What matters is knowing the inventory, not damaging it, and pricing future abatement into your offer.

### Can I skip the inspection if the house is from the late '90s?

Mostly yes for the classic legacy materials — then radon and moisture remain the relevant topics, and both are cheap to test. For anything older: the table sorts your suspicions, but only samples and lab analysis give certainty. One lab test costs less than an hour of your closing attorney's time.

### What about buried oil tanks, contaminated soil, and EMF?

An abandoned oil tank and legacy soil contamination (former commercial use!) are environmental rather than indoor-air issues but can get genuinely expensive — state environmental agencies keep searchable databases, and tank sweeps cost a few hundred dollars. Electric and magnetic fields are measurable but play in a far weaker evidence league than asbestos, lead, and radon.
