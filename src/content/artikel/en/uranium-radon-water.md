Water is a solvent — and what it dissolves on its way through rock depends on the geology. In granite and gneiss regions, that can include **uranium** and the radioactive gas **radon**. Both are entirely natural — and both deserve a closer look in specific situations.

## Uranium: chemistry beats physics

Uranium in drinking water regularly surprises people: the problem is less its (weak) radioactivity than its **chemical toxicity to the kidneys** — much like other heavy metals. The limits reflect that chemistry:

| Rule | Level |
| --- | --- |
| U.S. EPA Maximum Contaminant Level | 30 µg/L |
| WHO guideline value | 30 µg/L |
| Germany (drinking water ordinance) | 10 µg/L |
| EU bottled water labeled "suitable for infants" | max. 2 µg/L |

Public utilities must monitor and meet these values. Uranium becomes relevant mainly for **private wells** in affected regions — and for parents mixing infant formula: babies are the most sensitive group, hence the strict infant-water standard discussed under [Mineral water](https://everydayradiation.com/en/articles/mineral-water).

## Radon: from the water into the air

Radon-rich water matters mostly as a detour into indoor air: **showering, cooking, and laundry** release the gas from the water, adding to the radon already [seeping in from the soil](/en/articles/what-is-radon). Drinking radon is comparatively uncritical; inhaling it remains the number-one natural-radiation lung cancer issue. As a rule of thumb, 10,000 pCi/L of radon in water adds about 1 pCi/L to indoor air.

Noteworthy levels occur almost exclusively in **wells and springs in granite country** — New England is the classic U.S. example, which is why states like New Hampshire and Maine urge well owners to test. The EU applies a 100 Bq/L parameter for public supplies.

## What this means in practice

- **On a public system:** no action needed. Levels are monitored, exceedances are rare and reportable — check your annual Consumer Confidence Report.
- **Private well in granite/gneiss country:** test once for uranium and radon (specialty labs offer both, together roughly $60–150). For high radon, **aerating the water** before use already helps; for uranium, certified treatment (ion exchange, reverse osmosis) is the route.
- **Infant in a household on well water:** test for uranium before the first bottle — or switch to suitable bottled water.

And the closing perspective: for almost everyone, the radiation dose via drinking water is a footnote — **airborne radon** is the factor that counts. Check both in the right order: [air first](/en/articles/testing-for-radon), then water.
