Few environmental topics have hit the U.S. as hard as **PFAS** — per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, better known as **"forever chemicals."** Multibillion-dollar settlements against manufacturers, thousands of affected utilities, and in **April 2024 the EPA's first-ever federal drinking water limits** for them. Europe follows with EU-wide limits from January 2026.

## What are PFAS — and why "forever"?

PFAS are a family of thousands of industrial chemicals that repel water, grease, and dirt — which is why they spent decades in nonstick pans, rain jackets, food packaging, stain-proof carpets, and above all **firefighting foam**. Their strength is their problem: the carbon-fluorine bond is so stable that the substances **essentially never break down** in the environment. They accumulate in soil, water, and blood; the best-studied members (PFOA, PFOS) are linked to elevated cholesterol, reduced vaccine response in children, and — at high exposures — kidney and testicular cancer.

Typical hotspots: firefighter training grounds, airports, military bases, and certain industrial sites.

## The new rules

| Rule | Limit |
| --- | --- |
| U.S. EPA (2024): PFOA, PFOS individually | 4 ng/L (parts per trillion) |
| U.S. EPA (2024): PFHxS, PFNA, GenX individually | 10 ng/L |
| EU (from Jan 2026): "Sum of PFAS" (20 compounds) | 100 ng/L |
| EU (from Jan 2026): "PFAS total" | 500 ng/L |

The EPA limits are among the strictest in the world — utilities have until 2029 to comply, and monitoring data is already being published.

## How worried should you be?

For most households, measured tap-water levels sit **below the new limits** — PFAS is less a "panic at the faucet" issue than an environmental and hotspot issue. What makes sense:

1. **Check your utility:** U.S. systems have been testing under the EPA's UCMR program — results are public, and your annual Consumer Confidence Report will carry PFAS values going forward.
2. **Near a known hotspot** (training ground, base, industrial site in the news): ask specifically or order a lab test — consumer PFAS panels run about $100–300.
3. **Private well in a suspect area:** test, don't guess.

## Filtering — done right

Unlike some contaminants, PFAS actually responds well to **carbon-block filtration** and **reverse osmosis** — the same technology households in affected U.S. regions use, often with utility or settlement funding. The keys: choose certified products (NSF/ANSI 53 or 58 with an explicit PFAS reduction claim) and **replace cartridges religiously**, or the filter itself becomes the problem. And as always, the order from [Testing your tap water](/en/articles/testing-tap-water) applies: measure first, then filter.
