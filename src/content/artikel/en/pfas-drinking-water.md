Few environmental topics have hit the U.S. as hard as **PFAS** — per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, better known as **"forever chemicals."** Multibillion-dollar settlements against manufacturers like 3M and DuPont, thousands of affected utilities, contaminated bases and farm towns in the news — and in **April 2024 the EPA's first-ever federal drinking water limits** for them. Europe follows with EU-wide limits from January 2026. Time to sort the topic without alarmism: what are these chemicals, how do they get into your water — and when is it actually worth acting?

## What are PFAS — and why "forever"?

PFAS are a family of many thousands of industrial chemicals that repel water, grease, and dirt — which is why they spent decades in nonstick pans, rain jackets, stain-proof carpets, pizza boxes, cosmetics, and above all **firefighting foam**. Their strength is their problem: the carbon-fluorine bond is one of the most stable in organic chemistry. In the environment the substances **essentially never break down** — they travel with water, from the training ground into the aquifer, from sewage sludge onto fields, from the river into the water supply.

They linger in the body too: blood half-lives of the best-known members, PFOA and PFOS, are measured in years. Practically every American has measurable PFAS in their blood — the CDC's biomonitoring has shown that for decades. The question isn't whether, but how much.

**On the health side,** the strength of the evidence varies by compound. Well documented at elevated, long-term exposures: raised cholesterol, reduced vaccine response in children, lower birth weight, and liver enzyme changes. The International Agency for Research on Cancer has classified PFOA as **carcinogenic to humans** (Group 1) since 2023, PFOS as possibly carcinogenic; the landmark C8 studies around DuPont's West Virginia plant linked PFOA to kidney and testicular cancer. Important context: these findings come mostly from heavily contaminated regions and exposed workers — they don't mean background levels make people sick. But they explain why limits keep falling worldwide.

## Where PFAS comes from — the hotspot logic

PFAS in drinking water is not an everywhere-equally problem but a **hotspot problem**. Typical sources:

- **firefighter training grounds, airports, and military bases** — decades of drills with PFAS foam are the world's most common cause of contaminated groundwater plumes; hundreds of U.S. installations are under investigation
- **industrial sites** in paper, plating, textiles, and chemicals — the C8 contamination around Parkersburg, West Virginia became the template case (and the movie "Dark Waters")
- **farmland** where contaminated sewage sludge was spread — Maine has shut down farms over it
- and the water systems downstream of all of the above: EPA monitoring suggests **tens of millions of Americans** are served by systems that will need treatment to meet the 2024 limits

If you don't live near sites like these, your tap water is very likely unremarkable.

## The new rules

| Rule | Limit |
| --- | --- |
| U.S. EPA (2024): PFOA, PFOS individually | **4 ng/L** (parts per trillion) |
| U.S. EPA (2024): PFHxS, PFNA, GenX individually | 10 ng/L |
| EU (from Jan 2026): "Sum of PFAS" (20 compounds) | 100 ng/L |
| EU (from Jan 2026): "PFAS total" | 500 ng/L |

The EPA limits are among the strictest in the world — 4 parts per trillion is roughly one drop in twenty Olympic pools, near the edge of what labs can reliably measure. Utilities have until **2029** to comply (a 2025 EPA proposal would extend some deadlines and rework the rules for the non-PFOA/PFOS compounds — the core PFOA/PFOS limits stand). Monitoring data from thousands of systems is already public, and utilities exceeding limits must inform their customers and install treatment.

## How worried should you be?

For most households, measured tap-water levels sit **below the new limits** — PFAS is less a "panic at the faucet" issue than an environmental and hotspot issue. And for most people, a substantial share of PFAS intake comes through food (fish, some packaging) rather than water. A sensible three-step approach:

1. **Check your utility:** U.S. systems have been testing under the EPA's UCMR 5 program — results are public in the EPA's database, and your annual **Consumer Confidence Report** carries PFAS values going forward. A five-minute lookup answers the question conclusively for most people.
2. **Near a known hotspot** — training ground, base, industrial site in the news — ask your utility specifically or order a lab test: **consumer PFAS panels run about $100–300** at accredited labs. That's pricier than metals testing but appropriate for a genuine suspicion. Make sure the panel covers the EPA-regulated compounds.
3. **Private well in a suspect area:** test, don't guess. Wells fall outside every public monitoring program — there, you are your own utility ([more on well testing](/en/articles/testing-tap-water)). Near military sites, ask about DoD-funded testing and treatment programs before paying yourself.

## Filtering — done right

Unlike many dissolved contaminants, PFAS actually responds well to home treatment — the same technology households in affected U.S. regions use, often with utility or settlement funding:

- **Carbon-block filters** (under-sink or faucet-mount) hold back long-chain PFAS like PFOA and PFOS well; short-chain compounds break through sooner. The keys: a certification with an explicit PFAS reduction claim (**NSF/ANSI 53**) and **cartridge changes on schedule** — an exhausted filter can release what it caught.
- **Reverse osmosis** (**NSF/ANSI 58** with a PFAS claim) also removes short-chain PFAS reliably and is the most thorough household option — at the price of reject water and the removal of desirable minerals too.
- **Not suitable:** boiling (PFAS doesn't evaporate away), basic pitcher filters without a PFAS claim, water softeners.

And as always, the order from [Testing your tap water](/en/articles/testing-tap-water) applies: **measure first, then filter.** A filter against a problem your water doesn't have costs money every month and delivers nothing but maintenance duties.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I switch to bottled water?

As a PFAS strategy: no. Bottled water comes from the same environment and isn't systematically cleaner — the FDA has found PFAS in some bottled brands, and until recently bottled water had no PFAS standard at all. If a local finding makes you want a stopgap, fine — long-term, certified filters are the cheaper and cleaner answer.

### Do I need to throw out my nonstick pan?

No. Intact PTFE ("Teflon") coatings are chemically inert and don't shed meaningful PFAS in normal use. The historical problem was manufacturing (PFOA as a processing aid, phased out in the U.S. by 2015), not cooking. Replace scratched pans for practical reasons — and choose cast iron, stainless, or ceramic next time if you want out of the topic entirely.

### Can I get PFAS out of my body?

Slowly, yes — once intake drops, the body excretes PFAS over years. There's no legitimate "detox" that speeds it up; blood donation is the only intervention with documented effect on PFAS levels. What matters is the direction: cut intake (check your water, filter at hotspots, follow fish advisories for contaminated waters) — time does the rest.

### My utility reports values "below the detection limit." Does that mean zero?

No — it means below what the method reliably quantifies, typically a few parts per trillion. Practically, that's the best possible answer: well under every limit, nothing to do.

### Why not just ban PFAS altogether?

That's being attempted: the EU is weighing a restriction on essentially the whole class — one of the largest in its history — and U.S. states like Maine and Minnesota have enacted broad phase-outs of PFAS in products. The fight is over exceptions (medical devices, semiconductors, energy tech) and timelines. Until then: limits in water, bans on individual compounds, litigation funding cleanups — and a legacy in soil and groundwater that will accompany us for decades.
