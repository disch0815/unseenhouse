If your radon level is too high, here's the good news: it can almost always be lowered substantially — often starting with simple steps. Here are the options, from least to most effort.

## Free and immediate: ventilate properly

Regular, thorough airing dilutes the radon concentration — open windows wide several times a day, especially in bedrooms and on the lowest lived-in floor. It's not a permanent fix for seriously elevated levels, but it lowers exposure right away. One caution: don't leave basement windows permanently cracked when the basement is colder than the house — that can strengthen the stack effect that pulls radon out of the soil in the first place.

## Seal the entry points

Radon enters wherever the building touches the ground and isn't tight. Typical weak spots:

- cracks and joints in the slab and foundation walls
- pipe and cable penetrations
- sump pits without sealed covers
- dirt floors and unsealed crawl spaces

Sealing with flexible caulk, fitting a sealed sump cover, and putting a proper vapor barrier over a crawl space floor often helps a lot — especially when a few entry paths dominate.

## Change the pressure: the method that works best

For clearly elevated levels, the most reliable solution is to keep radon from entering at all. **Active sub-slab depressurization** — a pipe through the slab and a small inline fan that vents soil gas above the roof — is the standard mitigation system in North America. Installed by a certified mitigation contractor (NRPP/NRSB listed), it typically costs **$800–$2,500** and routinely cuts levels by 80–99%. Balanced ventilation systems (HRV/ERV) can help as a complement.

## Building new? Prevention is almost free

In new construction, radon protection costs next to nothing: a sealed, continuous slab, sealed penetrations, and — in radon-prone zones — a passive vent pipe roughed in under the slab that only needs a fan added later if tests come back high. Many U.S. jurisdictions already require radon-resistant construction; ask your builder about it early.

## Afterwards: retest

Every measure is only as good as its measured effect. After the work is done, [test again](/en/articles/testing-for-radon) — ideally with a long-term test over the next heating season, and every couple of years thereafter.
