First, the reassurance: municipal tap water in the U.S. and Europe is **among the most tightly regulated products there are.** Utilities test constantly, and every U.S. water system must publish an annual **Consumer Confidence Report** — look yours up before spending anything.

## The blind spot: the last few feet

Official oversight ends at your property line or meter. **The plumbing inside the building is the owner's responsibility** — and that's exactly where most problems arise: old lead pipes and lead solder, brass fixtures leaching lead or nickel, copper pipes with corrosive water, stagnation in rarely used lines. The utility's water can be perfect and still arrive contaminated at your faucet.

## Four good reasons to test

1. **Older home (pre-1986), plumbing never replaced:** suspicion of lead pipes or lead solder — the single most important reason to test, see [Lead in drinking water](/en/articles/lead-in-drinking-water).
2. **An infant in the household:** babies are sensitive to lead, copper, nitrate, and uranium. If you mix formula with tap water, a test buys certainty.
3. **A private well:** about one in seven U.S. households is on its own well — entirely their own responsibility. Experts recommend annual tests for bacteria and nitrate, plus a one-time panel for heavy metals and, depending on the region, [uranium and radon](/en/articles/uranium-radon-water).
4. **Concrete red flags:** metallic taste, greenish or brownish discoloration, unknown plumbing after buying a home.

## Which test fits?

- **Dip strips from the hardware store ($5–20):** rough orientation for hardness and pH — unsuitable for health questions.
- **Mail-in lab kits ($30–150):** fill the bottle, ship it, get a report. The sensible middle class for lead, copper, nickel & co. — use a state-certified lab (your health department keeps a list).
- **Certified sampling on site (from ~$200):** if the result needs to stand up legally — toward a landlord or in a real estate deal — a professional must take the sample.

**The one move that matters with every sample:** what counts is **first-draw water** — the water that sat in the pipes overnight. Testing only well-flushed water misses exactly the contamination you're looking for. Good test instructions require both.

## And water filters?

A filter is the answer to a proven problem, not the first step. Measure first, then filter specifically — otherwise you're subscribing to a problem you don't have, and a poorly maintained filter (bacterial growth!) creates a new one.
