Radon is an invisible, odorless, tasteless gas produced by the natural decay of uranium in soil and rock. It exists everywhere — more in some regions, less in others. Outdoors it dilutes instantly and is harmless. It only becomes a problem when it accumulates inside buildings. And that's exactly what it does: **for most people, radon is the single largest source of radiation exposure they'll receive in a year** — more than all medical imaging combined.

## Where radon comes from: the decay chain in the ground

Practically every soil and rock contains a small amount of uranium-238. It decays through several intermediate steps — including radium-226 — and at one point in this chain something special appears: **radon-222, a noble gas.** Unlike its solid predecessors, it doesn't stay locked in the rock. It escapes through pores and cracks and rises with the soil gas. Its half-life of **3.8 days** is just long enough to travel from the subsoil into a basement and build up there.

How much radon a site releases depends on two things: the **uranium content of the underlying rock** (granite, shale, and phosphate-rich formations lead the pack) and its **permeability** (gravel and fractured rock let far more gas through than dense clay). That's why the EPA's Zone 1 counties — where average indoor levels run highest — cluster across the Upper Midwest, the Appalachians, the Rocky Mountain states, and much of the Northeast. But these are probabilities, not guarantees: **elevated homes are found in every state, including "low-radon" Zone 3 counties.**

## How radon gets into a house

Radon enters wherever the building touches the ground and isn't airtight:

- **cracks and joints** in the slab and foundation walls
- **pipe and cable penetrations**
- **sump pits and floor drains**
- **crawl spaces and dirt-floor basements**

The driving force is physics: warm indoor air rises and creates a slight vacuum in the house — the **stack effect**. The building literally sucks in soil gas, most strongly in winter when the heat is on and windows stay closed. That's why readings in winter often run two to three times higher than in summer, and why basements and ground floors are most affected — concentrations drop noticeably with each floor you go up.

Two houses next door to each other can differ several-fold: construction year, condition of the slab, ventilation habits, and even the geology under the individual lot all play a role. **Your neighbor's test result tells you almost nothing about your own house.**

## Why radon is dangerous — the numbers

Radon itself is mostly exhaled again. Its short-lived **decay products**, however — isotopes of polonium, bismuth, and lead — attach to dust and aerosol particles, lodge in the airways when inhaled, and irradiate the tissue there as alpha emitters. Over years and decades, this raises the probability of lung cancer.

The magnitude is well established:

- The **EPA attributes about 21,000 lung cancer deaths per year in the United States** to radon — the second-leading cause of lung cancer after smoking, and the leading cause among people who have never smoked.
- The **WHO** estimates that, depending on the country, **3 to 14 percent of all lung cancers** trace back to radon, with risk rising about **16 percent per 100 Bq/m³** (roughly per 2.7 pCi/L) of long-term exposure — with no threshold below which nothing happens.
- Roughly **1 in 15 American homes** has a radon level at or above the EPA action level. In some Zone 1 counties it's closer to 1 in 3.

The combination with tobacco is especially unfavorable: radon and smoking multiply each other's effects, so smokers in high-radon homes carry many times the risk of either factor alone. Still, keep the time frame in mind: this is a **long-term risk built up over decades**. An afternoon in a basement or one winter in a high-radon rental is no reason to worry. What matters is where you live and sleep for years.

## Which levels are too high?

| Reference | Level | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EPA action level | **4 pCi/L** (≈ 148 Bq/m³) | Fix your home at or above this level |
| EPA "consider fixing" range | **2–4 pCi/L** | Mitigation often worthwhile |
| WHO reference level | **100 Bq/m³** (≈ 2.7 pCi/L) | Recommended target for risk minimization |
| Switzerland / Germany / Italy (existing homes) | 300 Bq/m³ (≈ 8 pCi/L) | European reference levels for comparison |

The action level is not a line between "safe" and "dangerous" — risk rises continuously with concentration, and the EPA itself notes there is no known safe level. 4 pCi/L marks the point where fixing the home is urgently recommended; the WHO's 100 Bq/m³ is the more ambitious target.

The [EPA Map of Radon Zones](https://www.epa.gov/radon) shows which U.S. counties tend to run high, and many state radon programs publish finer-grained data. But because radon is hyper-local, the map only tells you how curious to be — not what's in your house.

## What you can actually do

The good news: among cancer risks, radon may be the most fixable one. The path has three steps:

1. **Test** — the only way to know, since you can't see or smell radon. A qualified test kit costs $15–$30, a monitor a bit more. Everything you need: [Testing for radon](/en/articles/testing-for-radon).
2. **Interpret** — compare your long-term average against the action level, not individual daily spikes.
3. **Mitigate if needed** — from sealing entry points to an active sub-slab depressurization system, which a certified contractor typically installs for **$800–$2,500** and which reliably cuts levels by 80–99 percent. Details: [Reducing radon](/en/articles/reducing-radon).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is radon an issue in new homes too?

Yes — modern, tight construction protects the ground-contact side better but also seals the envelope, so radon that does get in escapes more slowly. Many states and the EPA promote radon-resistant new construction (passive vent stack, sealed slab penetrations) — worth requesting in any new build, and cheap to rough in during construction.

### I live on the third floor — does radon affect me?

Barely. Concentrations drop sharply with each floor; above the second story, elevated readings are rare. Exception: buildings where radon travels upward through elevator shafts, utility chases, or stairwells — if tests elsewhere in the building come back high, testing your unit is still worthwhile.

### Can I get radon from well water or building materials?

Both are possible but almost always secondary. Private well water in granite areas can carry radon that's released when you shower ([more here](/en/articles/uranium-radon-water)); a few building materials contribute locally. The main pathway is and remains soil gas.

### My neighbor tested at 1.5 pCi/L. Is that good enough for me?

No. Radon levels routinely differ several-fold between directly adjacent homes — because of construction, slab condition, and small-scale geology. Only your own test counts.

### Does a single high reading make me sick?

No. The risk comes from the **average exposure over years**. Short-term spikes — say, after an unventilated weekend — are normal and not an alarm signal. What matters is the long-term average.
