Radon is an invisible, odorless gas produced by the natural decay of uranium in soil and rock. It exists everywhere — more in some regions, less in others. Outdoors it dilutes instantly and is harmless. It only becomes a problem when it accumulates inside buildings.

## How does radon get into a house?

Radon rises from the ground and enters buildings through every gap it can find: cracks in the slab, joints, pipe and cable penetrations, sump pits, crawl spaces, unfinished dirt-floor basements. Because warm indoor air rises, a slight vacuum forms in the house that literally pulls the gas out of the soil — strongest in winter, when the heat is on and windows stay closed. Levels are usually highest in basements and on ground floors.

## Why is radon dangerous?

Radon itself is mostly exhaled again. Its short-lived decay products, however, attach to dust particles, lodge in the lungs, and irradiate the tissue there. The U.S. EPA attributes about **21,000 lung cancer deaths per year in the United States** to radon — making it the second-leading cause of lung cancer after smoking, a ranking the WHO confirms worldwide. Roughly **1 in 15 American homes** has an elevated radon level.

Important context: this is a long-term risk. Living for decades in rooms with high radon levels raises your lung cancer risk — especially in combination with smoking. A single afternoon in a basement is no reason to worry.

## Which levels are too high?

| Reference | Level |
| --- | --- |
| EPA action level (USA) | 4 pCi/L (≈ 148 Bq/m³) — fix your home |
| EPA "consider fixing" range | 2–4 pCi/L |
| WHO reference level | 100 Bq/m³ (≈ 2.7 pCi/L) |
| Switzerland / Germany / Italy (existing homes) | 300 Bq/m³ (≈ 8 pCi/L) |

The [EPA Map of Radon Zones](https://www.epa.gov/radon) shows which U.S. counties tend to run high — but radon is hyper-local: your neighbor's reading tells you little about your own house.

## First things first: test

You cannot see, smell, or taste radon — and levels vary drastically even between adjacent houses. The only way to know is to measure. How to do it, and what it costs, is covered in [Testing for radon](/en/articles/testing-for-radon).
