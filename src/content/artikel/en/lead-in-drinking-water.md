Flint, Michigan made it painfully clear: the utility's water can meet every standard at the treatment plant — and still **pick up lead in the last stretch into and inside your house**. The sources are lead service lines (the pipe connecting the water main to your home), lead solder (legal in the U.S. until 1986), and older brass fixtures. Your utility tests its network constantly; what happens between the curb and your kitchen faucet, nobody tests but you. An estimated **9 million lead service lines** are still in the ground across the country.

## Why lead is taken so seriously

Lead is a cumulative poison: the body barely excretes it and stores it in bones and tissue for years. Even in small amounts it harms **blood formation and the developing nervous system** — and decades of research have found no threshold below which lead is safely harmless. In children, even low blood lead levels are measurably associated with reduced cognitive development and behavioral problems.

Most vulnerable are **unborn children, infants, and toddlers**: their nervous systems are still developing, their gut absorbs lead far more readily than an adult's, and formula-fed infants drink more water per pound of body weight than anyone else in the household. The EPA and CDC agree there is **no known safe level of lead exposure for children** — which is why formula should never be mixed with water from lead plumbing. For adults the risk is lower — hypertension and kidney strain are the relevant long-term effects — but chronic exposure remains simply pointless.

## The rules are tightening

The EPA's **Lead and Copper Rule Improvements, finalized in October 2024**, are the biggest overhaul in three decades. Water systems must **replace virtually all lead service lines within 10 years** (a clock that starts in 2027), the action level drops from 15 to **10 µg/L**, and every system has to **inventory its service lines and tell customers what their line is made of**. Practically, that means: you can ask your utility about your line today, and many cities publish searchable maps. Systems with lead lines must also provide filters in some circumstances and notify households annually.

Europe is on the same path — the EU cuts its lead limit to 5 µg/L at the tap by 2036, and Germany requires remaining lead pipes removed by 2026. In short: **lead pipes are on their way out everywhere — but millions are still in the ground, and the decade of digging is only starting.**

## Spotting lead plumbing

- **Year built:** U.S. homes before 1986 may have lead solder on copper pipes; actual lead service lines cluster in pre-1960s neighborhoods, especially in the Midwest and Northeast. Partial renovations often replaced only what was visible — the service line under the front yard can be original.
- **The scratch test:** find where the water line enters the house (basement or crawl space, before the meter or shutoff valve). Exposed lead pipe is dull gray, soft — a key or screwdriver scratches it shiny silver — non-magnetic, and often ends in a characteristic bulb-shaped "wiped" joint. For comparison: steel is magnetic and hard, copper reddish, plastic obvious.
- **Ask your utility:** under the new rules it must know and tell you. A two-minute lookup or phone call can settle the question for free.
- **When in doubt: measure.** A certified lab test for lead costs **$20–60**, often bundled with other metals (copper, nickel, cadmium) for little more — the practical how-to is in [Testing your tap water](/en/articles/testing-tap-water). Crucial: take a **first-draw sample** — water that sat in the pipes overnight or for at least six hours, because that's exactly what you drink in the morning. A sample after long flushing measures the utility's water, not your plumbing. Many utilities and health departments offer **free lead testing** for homes with suspected lead lines — ask before you pay.

One note on the second-most-common culprit: **older brass fixtures and lead solder** also leach lead — in smaller amounts than a lead service line, but measurably. "Lead-free" brass was only tightened to 0.25 percent lead in 2014. If your test shows mildly elevated numbers and no lead line, fixtures and solder are the usual suspects.

## What helps immediately — and what actually solves it

**Immediate measure: flush.** In the morning and after long absences, **run the cold tap until the water turns noticeably colder** — typically 30 seconds to 2 minutes. That clears the stagnant water from your home's plumbing. Don't use first-draw water for drinking, cooking, coffee, or formula; it's fine for plants and cleaning. Use **only cold water** for anything you'll consume — hot water dissolves more lead from pipes, and boiling doesn't remove lead, it slightly concentrates it. For formula with confirmed lead plumbing, switch to filtered or bottled water consistently.

**Bridge solution: filter.** An **NSF/ANSI 53-certified filter for lead** — pitcher, faucet-mount, or under-sink — reliably cuts lead at the tap and is what health departments hand out in affected cities. Two caveats: cartridges must be changed on schedule (an exhausted filter can dump what it caught), and a filter manages the problem without fixing the cause.

**The real fix: replacing the lead line.** Everything else is managing an avoidable risk. Under the 2024 rules, utilities must replace their portion of the line within the 10-year window — ask about the schedule, and about **cost-sharing or full-replacement programs for the private portion**; many cities and several states now cover it entirely, and partial replacements (which can temporarily spike lead) are being phased out. For renters: a documented reading above the action level is your strongest lever with a landlord, and local health departments can compel action. Homeowners replacing plumbing should also swap pre-2014 brass fixtures — it's cheap while the walls are open.

## Frequently asked questions

### How would I notice lead in my water?

You wouldn't — that's the treacherous part. Lead in water is tasteless, odorless, and colorless, even at concentrations far above the action level. The only certainty is a lab test.

### Does boiling water help against lead?

No — the opposite. Boiling kills germs, but metals stay behind; as water evaporates, the lead concentration actually rises slightly. Against lead, only flushing, certified filters, and pipe replacement work.

### We have a lead service line and I'm pregnant — how urgent is this?

Act consistently, without panic: from today, take drinking and cooking water only through an NSF/ANSI 53 lead-certified filter (or bottled), get a first-draw lab test, and get on your utility's replacement schedule. Past normal use isn't a reason for anxiety beyond what you can change now — what matters is minimizing intake from today. Your doctor can order a simple blood lead test for peace of mind.

### My house was built in 1995 — can I close the topic?

Lead service line: practically yes. But brass fixtures made before 2014 can still leach some lead, aggressive water can pull copper from pipes, and private wells add their own list. If you want certainty, run the metals panel once — then the topic is genuinely closed.

### Who pays for the test and the replacement?

Increasingly: not you. Many utilities test for free where lead lines are suspected, the utility must replace its side of the line under the 2024 rules, and a growing number of cities and states fund the private side too — billions in federal infrastructure money are earmarked for exactly this. A call to your utility and a look at your city's lead program page come before any invoice.
